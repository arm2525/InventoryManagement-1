Theory Essentials: Derivation & Analysis

Duration: 45 minutes References: Harris (1913); Wilson (1934); Hadley & Whitin (1963)

The Economic Order Quantity (EOQ) model is the foundation of modern inventory control. Its value is not in its perfect reflection of reality—it is a significant oversimplification. Its value lies in its elegant mathematical formulation of the central tradeoff in inventory management:

  • Cost of Ordering: Placing many small orders incurs high fixed setup costs.
  • Cost of Holding: Placing one large order minimizes setup costs but incurs high holding costs (capital, storage, obsolescence).

The EOQ finds the precise quantity $Q^*$ that minimizes the total cost function.

1.1 Notation

We define the following parameters:

$$D = Annual demand (units/year)$$

$$K$$ = Fixed ordering cost per order ($/order)
$$h$$ = Annual holding cost per unit ($/unit/year)
$$c$$ = Unit purchase cost ($/unit)

1.2 Calculus-Based Derivation

We build a function for the Total Annual Relevant Cost, $$TRC(Q)$$, and find the $$Q$$ that minimizes it.

Step 1: Define Total Annual Ordering Cost

Number of orders per year = $$D/Q$$.

Total Annual Ordering Cost = $$(Number of orders) \times (Cost per order) = \frac{D}{Q} K$$

Step 2: Define Total Annual Holding Cost

The inventory level follows a sawtooth pattern from $Q$ to 0. The average inventory level is $$\frac{Q}{2}$$.

Total Annual Holding Cost = $$(Average inventory) \times (Holding cost per unit/year) = \frac{Q}{2} h$$

Step 3: Define Total Annual Relevant Cost (TRC)

The total annual purchase cost ($$D \times c$$) is constant (assuming no discounts) and can be ignored for optimization. The $TRC$ is:

$$TRC(Q) = \frac{DK}{Q} + \frac{Qh}{2}$$

Step 4: Find the Minimum (First Derivative)

Set the first derivative with respect to $Q$ to zero.

$$\frac{d}{dQ}TRC(Q) = \frac{d}{dQ} \left( DK Q^{-1} + \frac{h}{2} Q \right) = - \frac{DK}{Q^2} + \frac{h}{2}$$

$$- \frac{DK}{Q^2} + \frac{h}{2} = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad Q^2 = \frac{2DK}{h}$$

This gives the optimal $Q$, which we call $Q^*_0$ (the classic EOQ):

$$Q^*_0 = \sqrt{\frac{2DK}{h}}$$

Step 5: Confirm Convexity (Second Derivative)

We confirm this is a minimum by checking the second derivative:

$$\frac{d^2}{dQ^2}TRC(Q) = \frac{d}{dQ} \left( -DK Q^{-2} + \frac{h}{2} \right) = \frac{2DK}{Q^3}$$

Since $$D$$, $$K$$, and $$Q$$ are all positive, $$\frac{2DK}{Q^3} > 0$$. The cost function is convex, and $$Q^*$$ is the unique global minimum.

Ph.D. Level Analysis: Assumptions & Robustness

2.1 Core Assumptions

The model's simplicity relies on a strict set of assumptions. Violating them is the basis for all advanced inventory theory.

Key Assumptions

  • Demand: Deterministic, constant, and continuous. (Violated by stochasticity $\rightarrow$ Safety Stock)
  • Replenishment: Instantaneous (Lead time = 0). (Violated $\rightarrow$ Reorder Points)
  • Costs: Known, fixed, and stationary. (Violated $\rightarrow$ Quantity Discounts)
  • Horizon: Infinite and stationary. (Violated by non-stationary demand $\rightarrow$ Lot Sizing)
  • Constraints: No backorders, no capacity limits. (Violated $\rightarrow$ Backorder Models)

2.2 Mathematical Proof of Robustness

The EOQ model's most powerful feature is its robustness to parameter errors. The total cost curve is "flat" around the optimum $$Q^*$$. Let's define a cost penalty ratio $$\frac{TRC(Q')}{TRC(Q^*)}$$ where $Q'$ is a non-optimal quantity ($$Q' = \beta Q^*$$).

$$TRC(Q') = \frac{DK}{Q'} + \frac{Q'h}{2} = \frac{DK}{\beta Q^*} + \frac{\beta Q^* h}{2}$$

At the optimum $$Q^*$$, we know the ordering and holding costs are equal: $$\frac{DK}{Q^*} = \frac{Q^*h}{2} = \frac{TRC(Q^*)}{2}$$. Substitute these into the $$TRC(Q')$$ equation:

$$TRC(Q') = \frac{1}{\beta} \left( \frac{DK}{Q^*} \right) + \beta \left( \frac{Q^* h}{2} \right) = \frac{1}{\beta} \left( \frac{TRC(Q^*)}{2} \right) + \beta \left( \frac{TRC(Q^*)}{2} \right)$$

This gives the famous robustness formula:

$$\frac{TRC(Q')}{TRC(Q^*)} = \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{1}{\beta} + \beta \right)$$

Example: If your estimate for $$K$$ is 50% too high, your $$Q'$ will be $$\sqrt{1.5} \approx 1.22$ times $Q^*$ (so $\beta=1.22$). Your cost penalty is $\frac{1}{2}(\frac{1}{1.22} + 1.22) = 1.02$. A 50% parameter error leads to only a 2% cost penalty.

Key Model Extensions

3.1 EOQ with Planned Shortages (Backorders)

We relax the "no stockouts" assumption, incurring a backorder cost $b$ per unit per year. Let $S$ be the maximum inventory level and $Q$ the order quantity, so $(Q-S)$ is the maximum backorder level.

New Cost Function $TRC(Q, S)$

Annual Holding Cost = $\frac{S^2 h}{2Q}$

Annual Shortage Cost = $\frac{(Q-S)^2 b}{2Q}$

$$TRC(Q, S) = \frac{DK}{Q} + \frac{S^2 h}{2Q} + \frac{(Q-S)^2 b}{2Q}$$

Optimal Solution (Partial Derivatives)

Taking $\frac{\partial TRC}{\partial S} = 0$ gives the optimal $S^*$ in terms of $Q$:

$$S^* = Q \left( \frac{b}{h+b} \right)$$

Plugging this $S^*$ back in and taking $\frac{dTRC}{dQ} = 0$ yields the optimal $Q^*_b$:

$$Q^*_b = \sqrt{\frac{2DK}{h}} \sqrt{\frac{h+b}{b}}$$

3.2 EOQ with All-Units Quantity Discounts

Here, the unit cost $c_j$ decreases at price break $q_j$. This is complex because $h = i \times c_j$ and the total purchase cost $D c_j$ must be included.

$$TAC_j(Q) = \frac{DK}{Q} + \frac{Q(i c_j)}{2} + D c_j$$

This creates a set of discontinuous cost curves. The optimal solution is found via the following algorithm:

  1. Start with the lowest price $c_m$. Calculate its $Q^*_m = \sqrt{2DK / (i c_m)}$.
  2. If $Q^*_m$ is feasible (i.e., $Q^*_m \ge q_m$): This $Q^*_m$ is a candidate. Calculate $TAC_m(Q^*_m)$.
  3. If $Q^*_m$ is not feasible (i.e., $Q^*_m < q_m$): The best cost for this price level is at $q_m$. $q_m$ is a candidate. Calculate $TAC_m(q_m)$.
  4. Repeat for all other price levels $j < m$.
  5. The optimal order quantity is the candidate $Q$ (either a $Q^*_j$ or a $q_j$) with the minimum Total Annual Cost ($TAC$).
