Theory Essentials: The Dynamic Lot Sizing (DLS) Problem

Duration: 45 Minutes References: Wagner & Whitin (1958); Silver & Meal (1973)

The EOQ and $(Q, R)$ models assume demand is stationary (constant, or random around a constant mean). This assumption is violated in many real-world scenarios, such as:

  • Seasonality: Demand for winter coats is high in autumn and zero in summer.
  • MRP Systems: The "demand" for a component is "lumpy"—it's zero for three weeks, then 500 units are needed for a production run.

The Dynamic Lot Sizing (DLS) problem addresses this. We have a finite planning horizon $T$ (e.g., 12 weeks), and the demand $d_t$ for each period $t = 1, ..., T$ is known but variable.

1.1 Formal Problem Definition

We define the following parameters (assuming costs are stationary for simplicity):

  • $T$ = Number of periods in the planning horizon.
  • $d_t$ = Known demand in period $t$.
  • $K$ = Fixed ordering cost per order placed.
  • $h$ = Holding cost to carry one unit from period $t$ to $t+1$.

And the decision variables:

  • $Q_t$ = Order quantity in period $t$.
  • $I_t$ = Inventory level at the *end* of period $t$.
  • $\delta_t$ = A binary variable: 1 if $Q_t > 0$, 0 otherwise.

The objective is to find an ordering plan $\{Q_1, Q_2, ..., Q_T\}$ that minimizes total cost:

$$ \min Z = \sum_{t=1}^T (K \cdot \delta_t + h \cdot I_t) $$

Subject to (the inventory balance equation):

$$ I_{t-1} + Q_t - I_t = d_t \quad \text{for all } t=1...T $$

(Assuming $I_0 = 0$ and $I_t, Q_t \ge 0$)

2. The Optimal Solution: Wagner-Whitin (WW) Algorithm

This problem could be solved as a mixed-integer program, but in their seminal 1958 paper, Wagner and Whitin developed a much more efficient $O(T^2)$ dynamic programming solution. This algorithm is built on two powerful properties.

Property 1: The Zero-Inventory Property (ZIP)

An optimal policy will only place an order in period $t$ if the starting inventory $I_{t-1} = 0$.

Proof (by contradiction): Imagine an optimal policy orders $Q_t > 0$ when $I_{t-1} > 0$. That inventory $I_{t-1}$ must have come from an order in some period $k < t$. We could simply *increase* the order in period $k$ to cover period $t$'s demand, and set $Q_t = 0$. This would save the ordering cost $K$ in period $t$ and *at worst* incur the same holding costs (or less). Thus, the original policy could not have been optimal.

The ZIP implies that in any period $t$, the demand $d_t$ is met either by a new order $Q_t$ or by inventory carried over from a *previous* order, but never both.

Property 2: The Planning Horizon Theorem

If, in an optimal plan, it is best to place an order in period $t^*$ to satisfy demand for period $k$ (where $t^* \le k$), then the optimal plan for periods $1, ..., t^*-1$ is independent of the demands $d_t$ for $t \ge k$.

This property is what allows the DP to work. It proves we can make decisions for the early periods without needing to look at all future demands simultaneously.

2.1 The DP Formulation (Forward Pass)

Let $f_t$ = The minimum possible cost to satisfy demand for periods $1, 2, ..., t$.

To calculate $f_t$, we use the ZIP. The demand $d_t$ must have been satisfied by an order placed in some period $j \le t$. This order also covered all demand for periods $j, j+1, ..., t-1$.

We can find $f_t$ by considering all possible "last order" periods $j$:

$$ f_t = \min_{1 \le j \le t} \{ f_{j-1} + C_{jt} \} $$

Where $f_0 = 0$ and $C_{jt}$ is the total cost of ordering in period $j$ to satisfy all demand from $j$ through $t$.

$C_{jt}$ = (Ordering Cost in $j$) + (Holding costs for $d_{j+1}, ..., d_t$).

$$ C_{jt} = K + h \cdot [ (1)d_{j+1} + (2)d_{j+2} + ... + (t-j)d_t ] $$

The algorithm works by finding $f_1$, then $f_2$, and so on, up to $f_T$. The final $f_T$ is the optimal cost for the entire horizon. To find the plan, we trace back which $j$ was chosen at each step $t$.

3. Practical Heuristics (for $O(T^2)$ is too slow)

The WW algorithm is optimal, but computationally intensive for large $T$. In practice (e.g., in most MRP/ERP systems), simpler, myopic heuristics are used.

3.1 Silver-Meal (SM) Heuristic

  • Logic: "Minimize the average cost *per period* for the current order."
  • Method: We are in period $t$ (with $I_{t-1}=0$). We must order. How many periods ($n$) should this order cover?
  • Let $C(n)$ = Cost of ordering in $t$ to cover $n$ periods (from $t$ to $t+n-1$).
  • $C(n) = K + h \cdot [ (0)d_t + (1)d_{t+1} + ... + (n-1)d_{t+n-1} ]$
  • $AC(n) = C(n) / n$ (Average cost per period)
  • Rule: Keep adding period $t+n$ to the order as long as $AC(n+1) < AC(n)$. Stop and order to cover $n$ periods when the average cost per period starts to increase.

3.2 Part-Period Balancing (PPB)

  • Logic: "Order enough to make the total holding cost *approximately equal* to the ordering cost." (This is a dynamic version of the EOQ's core tradeoff).
  • Method: We are in period $t$. We must order. Find the number of periods $n$ to cover such that the total holding cost $H(n)$ is as close as possible to the setup cost $K$.
  • $H(n) = h \cdot [ (0)d_t + (1)d_{t+1} + ... + (n-1)d_{t+n-1} ]$
  • Rule: Find $n$ such that $H(n) \approx K$. (In practice, find $n$ that minimizes $|H(n) - K|$).
