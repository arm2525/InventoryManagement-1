Theory Essentials: Modeling Stochastic Demand

Duration: 1.0 Hour References: Hadley & Whitin (1963); Scarf (1958); Zipkin (2000)

In Module 1.1, we operated in a deterministic world. We now relax the most restrictive assumption: that demand is constant and known. When demand is stochastic (random), the EOQ model is insufficient because it provides no protection against stockouts.

The core problem is uncertainty during the replenishment lead time. If a customer order arrives while we are waiting for our replenishment, we risk a stockout. We buffer against this risk using Safety Stock (SS).

1.1 Modeling Demand During Lead Time (DDLT)

We must model the demand that occurs during the replenishment lead time. Let:

  • $L$ = Replenishment lead time (assumed constant for now, in periods)
  • $d$ = Average demand per period (e.g., per week)
  • $\sigma_d$ = Standard deviation of demand per period
  • $\mu_{DDLT}$ = Average (mean) demand during lead time
  • $\sigma_{DDLT}$ = Standard deviation of demand during lead time

If demand in each period is independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.), the parameters for DDLT are:

$$\mu_{DDLT} = d \times L$$

$$\sigma_{DDLT} = \sigma_d \times \sqrt{L}$$

This "square-root of L" relationship is fundamental. It holds that variability (risk) does not scale linearly with time, but with the square root of time.

1.2 Service Level Metrics: The Ph.D. Distinction

How much safety stock should we hold? This depends on our desired service level. At a graduate level, it is critical to distinguish between the two primary types of service metrics.

Type 1: Cycle Service Level (CSL)

Also known as the "Alpha ($\alpha$) Service Level".

  • Definition: The probability of not stocking out in any given replenishment cycle.
  • Example: A 95% CSL means that 95 out of 100 replenishment cycles will be completed without any customer backorders.
  • Focus: Frequency of stockout events.

Type 2: Fill Rate (FR)

Also known as the "Beta ($\beta$) Service Level".

  • Definition: The fraction of total demand that is satisfied directly from stock (i.e., not backordered or lost).
  • Example: A 99% Fill Rate means that 99 out of every 100 units demanded are shipped immediately.
  • Focus: Magnitude (volume) of stockout events.

Why the distinction matters:

A 95% CSL is much less expensive to provide than a 95% Fill Rate. The CSL allows for a (rare) stockout to be catastrophically large, whereas the Fill Rate explicitly limits the total volume of shortage. Most managers say* CSL ("I want to avoid stockouts") but mean* FR ("I want to ship almost all my orders").

2. Continuous Review $(Q, R)$ Policy

This is the most common stochastic inventory policy, also known as a "fixed order quantity" or "two-bin" system.

The $(Q, R)$ Policy

Monitor the Inventory Position (IP) continuously.
When $IP \le R$ (the Reorder Point), place an order for a fixed quantity $Q$.

Inventory Position (IP) = (On-hand Inventory) + (On-order Inventory) - (Backorders)

2.1 Finding $(Q, R)$ for a target CSL

This is the simpler case. The two parameters $Q$ and $R$ can be found (almost) independently.

Step 1: Calculate $Q$

We use the standard $Q^*_0 = \sqrt{2DK/h}$ from Module 1.1. This is an approximation (as the stockout cost is not included), but it is the standard method.

Step 2: Calculate $R$ for target CSL

We want the probability of DDLT being less than or equal to $R$ to be our CSL. $$CSL = P(DDLT \le R)$$

Assuming DDLT is Normal($\mu_{DDLT}, \sigma_{DDLT}$), we standardize. The $z$-score is the number of standard deviations we need:

$$CSL = P \left( Z \le \frac{R - \mu_{DDLT}}{\sigma_{DDLT}} \right)$$

Let $z = \Phi^{-1}(CSL)$ (i.e., the inverse standard normal of the CSL, or `NORM.S.INV(CSL)`).

This gives us the formula for $R$:

$$R = \mu_{DDLT} + z \cdot \sigma_{DDLT}$$
Where Safety Stock (SS) = $z \cdot \sigma_{DDLT}$

2.2 Finding $(Q, R)$ for a target Fill Rate (FR)

This is significantly more complex, as $Q$ and $R$ are now interdependent. The expected shortage in a cycle depends on $R$, and the annual cost impact of that shortage depends on how many cycles there are (which depends on $Q$).

We define $E(S \text{ per cycle})$ as the Expected Shortage per Replenishment Cycle. This is the average number of units we are short by, *given that a stockout occurs*, times the probability of a stockout.

For a Normal distribution, this is given by the Standard Normal Loss Function, $G(z)$.

$$E(S \text{ per cycle}) = \sigma_{DDLT} \cdot G(z) \quad \text{where } z = \frac{R - \mu_{DDLT}}{\sigma_{DDLT}}$$

$$G(z) = \phi(z) - z(1 - \Phi(z))$$

The Fill Rate is $FR = 1 - \frac{\text{Annual Shortage}}{\text{Annual Demand}} = 1 - \frac{E(S \text{ per cycle}) \times (\text{Cycles per year})}{D}$. Since Cycles per year = $D/Q$, we get:

$$FR = 1 - \frac{E(S \text{ per cycle})}{Q} = 1 - \frac{\sigma_{DDLT} \cdot G(z)}{Q}$$

This gives us an equation that links $Q$ and $z$ (and thus $R$):

$$G(z) = \frac{Q (1 - FR)}{\sigma_{DDLT}}$$

This requires an iterative procedure to find the *simultaneously optimal* $(Q, R)$ if a shortage cost $p$ ($/unit) is known. However, if $FR$ is a constraint, the standard procedure is:

  1. Calculate $Q = EOQ$.
  2. Using this $Q$, calculate the required $G(z) = \frac{Q (1 - FR)}{\sigma_{DDLT}}$.
  3. Find $z$ from $G(z)$ (using a table or formula).
  4. Calculate $R = \mu_{DDLT} + z \cdot \sigma_{DDLT}$.

3. Periodic Review $(P, S)$ Policy

This is the alternative to continuous review, also known as a "fixed order interval" or "order-up-to" system.

The $(P, S)$ Policy

Review the Inventory Position (IP) at fixed, periodic intervals of $P$.
Place an order for a variable quantity $Q = S - IP$, where $S$ is the "Order-Up-To Level".

The key difference here is the risk period. In a $(Q, R)$ policy, you are only vulnerable during the lead time $L$. In a $(P, S)$ policy, you place an order and are then vulnerable for the entire time* until the next* order arrives, which is $P + L$.

Deriving the Order-Up-To Level ($S$)

We must set $S$ high enough to cover all demand during the risk period ($P+L$) to achieve our desired CSL.

$$\mu_{P+L} = d \times (P+L)$$

$$\sigma_{P+L} = \sigma_d \times \sqrt{P+L}$$

To achieve a target CSL, we find $z = \Phi^{-1}(CSL)$ as before.

$$S = \mu_{P+L} + z \cdot \sigma_{P+L}$$

The safety stock is $SS = z \cdot \sigma_{P+L}$. Since $\sqrt{P+L} > \sqrt{L}$, a periodic review policy will always carry more safety stock than a continuous review policy for the same CSL, assuming all else is equal.

4. Ph.D. Level Analysis: Distribution-Free Models

Scarf's Rule (1958)

A major assumption in our models is that DDLT is *Normally* distributed. What if it isn't? What if we only know its mean ($\mu$) and standard deviation ($\sigma$)?

Herbert Scarf (1958) tackled this using a min-max approach: find the policy that minimizes the maximum possible cost, over all possible (unimodal) distributions with that $\mu$ and $\sigma$.

This is effectively a Newsvendor problem (Module 1.4) where the distribution is unknown. Scarf proved that the min-max optimal stock level $R^*$ is the solution to:

$$R^* = \mu + \frac{\sigma}{2} \left( \sqrt{\frac{p}{h}} - \sqrt{\frac{h}{p}} \right)$$

(Where $p$ is unit shortage cost and $h$ is unit holding cost)

The key insight is that this robust, distribution-free policy requires holding safety stock ($R^* - \mu$) that is linearly* proportional to the standard deviation ($\sigma$), not a multiple ($z$) of it. This provides a crucial benchmark for high-uncertainty, low-information environments.
