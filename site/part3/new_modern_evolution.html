Course Synthesis: The Evolution of the "Problem"

Duration: 1.5 Hours References: All course materials

We have completed a 10.5-hour journey through a century of research. The single most important takeaway for a Ph.D. student is this: the field of inventory management is not a static set of solved problems, but a dynamic, evolving conversation.

We have seen the "problem" of inventory evolve:

  • 1910s (Harris): The problem is *economic*. (Minimizing a known cost).
  • 1960s (Scarf): The problem is *mathematical*. (Proving optimality in a stochastic world).
  • 1990s (Lee): The problem is *informational*. (Fixing the distortion of information).
  • 2000s (Cachon): The problem is *incentive-based*. (Fixing the misaligned goals of rational agents).
  • 2020s (You): The problem is *behavioral, sustainable, resilient, and data-driven*.

The final step in this course is for you to join this conversation. This is done by writing a research paper.

The Anatomy of a Research Contribution

Your final workshop is to develop a proposal for an original research paper. All publishable papers in this field share a common structure. Your proposal should have one paragraph for each of these five sections.

1. The Introduction (The "Hook")

  • What is the problem? (e.g., "Food delivery platforms need to position drivers, but demand is uncertain.")
  • Why does it matter? (e.g., "Poor positioning leads to high wait times and lost customers, costing millions.")
  • What do you do? (e.g., "We develop a stochastic-dynamic model...").
  • What do you find? (e.g., "...that our new policy reduces costs by 15% over the myopic heuristic.")

2. The Literature Review (The "Gap")

  • What have others done? (The "Frontier"): (e.g., "Classical inventory theory (Scarf 1960) solved the static problem. On-demand research (Stream 3) has looked at...").
  • What is the "Gap"? (e.g., "However, no model has yet integrated real-time ML demand updates with the formal DP model for driver inventory.")

3. The Model (The "Math")

  • What are your assumptions? (e.g., "We assume a finite horizon $T$, Poisson demand...")
  • What is your formulation? (e.g., "We formulate this as an MDP, $f_n(x) = \min \{ ... \}$.")
  • What is your methodology? (e.g., "We prove the optimality of a new state-dependent (s,S) policy." or "We use SAA and empirical data...").

4. The Analysis (The "Results")

  • What did you find? (e.g., "Our numerical analysis shows our policy (Policy A) outperforms the benchmark (Policy B) under high demand volatility.")
  • Why? (The "Insight"): (e.g., "This is because Policy A better accounts for...").

5. The Conclusion (The "So What?")

  • What is the *managerial* takeaway? (e.g., "Our model suggests managers should stop using simple forecasts and use...").
  • What are the limitations & future research? (e.g., "We did not model... Future research should...")

Final Capstone Workshop: Your Research Proposal

Activity: Prepare a 1-page Research Proposal.
  1. Select a Topic: Choose a topic from the "Frontier" module (Stream 3.2) or a novel combination (e.g., "Behavioral + Resilience").
  2. Identify a "Gap": What is the *one* specific, unanswered question you will address?
  3. Propose a Methodology: How will you answer it? (e_g., "I will build on the Clark & Scarf (1960) model to include...").
  4. State your "Contribution": What will the field learn from your paper that it does not already know?
  5. Present your proposal to the class for peer review and discussion.

This proposal is the culmination of the course. It demonstrates you can move from *consuming* research to *creating* it.
