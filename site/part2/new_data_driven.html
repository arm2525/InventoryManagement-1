Theory Essentials: From Parameters to Data

Duration: 1.5 Hours References: Levi, Rudi, & Kallus (2015); Bertsimas & Thiele (2006)

All the models we have studied (EOQ, Newsvendor, (Q,R)) share a fundamental assumption: we know the model parameters. We assume we know $D$, or we know the *exact* demand distribution $D \sim N(\mu, \sigma)$.

In reality, we have data. The standard, but flawed, approach is **"Estimate, then Optimize"** (also called "plug-in").

  1. Step 1 (Estimate): Use historical data to fit a distribution. (e.g., find $\hat{\mu} = 50, \hat{\sigma} = 10$).
  2. Step 2 (Optimize): Plug these *estimates* into the formula as if they are *truth*. (e.g., $Q^* = \hat{\mu} + z \hat{\sigma}$).

The critical flaw is that this approach **ignores estimation error**. The optimal decision for an *estimated* distribution is not necessarily the optimal decision for the *true* underlying problem. This leads to brittle and systematically biased policies, especially with limited data.

2. The Non-Parametric Approach: Sample Average Approximation (SAA)

If we don't want to assume a parametric form (like $Normal$), we can use the data itself as the distribution. SAA, also called the "empirical" method, is a powerful and robust alternative.

Instead of solving the stochastic problem $\min_Q \mathbb{E}[Cost(Q, D)]$, we solve the *deterministic* problem based on our $N$ data points $d_1, ..., d_N$:

$$ \min_Q \left\{ \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N Cost(Q, d_i) \right\} $$

**Example: The SAA Newsvendor**

The classical Newsvendor solution is $F(Q^*) = CR$, where $F(d)$ is the *assumed* CDF. The SAA solution is $F_N(Q^*) = CR$, where $F_N(d)$ is the **empirical CDF**.

SAA Newsvendor Solution

Let your $N$ historical demands be $d_1, ..., d_N$. Sort them: $d_{(1)} \le d_{(2)} \le ... \le d_{(N)}$.

Let $CR = \frac{C_u}{C_o+C_u}$ be the critical ratio.

Find the index $k = \lceil CR \cdot N \rceil$.

The SAA optimal order quantity is $Q^* = d_{(k)}$.

This is simple, requires no distributional assumptions, and is provably robust.

3. The "Prescriptive Analytics" (Integrated) Approach

This is the modern research frontier. What if we have *features* (or "covariates") for each demand point? For example, $x = (\text{weather, day of week, holiday, price})$.

The "Estimate, then Optimize" approach would:

  1. Build an ML model (e.g., Random Forest) to get a forecast $\hat{\mu}(x)$.
  2. Plug $\hat{\mu}(x)$ into a Newsvendor formula $Q(x) = \hat{\mu}(x) + z\sigma$.

The flaw is that the ML model is trained to minimize *forecast error* (e.g., $\min \mathbb{E}[(D - \hat{D})^2]$), not to minimize *decision cost* (e.g., $\min \mathbb{E}[Cost(Q(x), D)]$ ).

The **prescriptive approach** (e.g., in papers by Levi, Rudi, Kallus, or Bertsimas) builds a model that *directly* optimizes the decision. It seeks a policy $\pi(x) \rightarrow Q$ that minimizes the *end-to-end* Newsvendor cost, using techniques like stochastic gradient descent on the cost function itself. This "cost-sensitive" learning is far more powerful.

Workshop: SAA vs. Plug-in

Scenario: You are a Newsvendor with $C_u = 8$ and $C_o = 2$.
You have 10 historical demand data points: $\{15, 22, 28, 31, 35, 39, 44, 48, 55, 60\}$.
  1. Calculate the Critical Ratio $CR$.
  2. Policy A (SAA): Use the SAA method to find the optimal order quantity $Q^*_{SAA}$ from the *sorted data* and the $CR$.
  3. Policy B (Plug-in): Calculate the sample mean ($\hat{\mu} = 37.7$) and sample std. dev. ($\hat{\sigma} \approx 14.5$) from the data. Assume $D \sim N(\hat{\mu}, \hat{\sigma})$ and calculate the "plug-in" optimal quantity $Q^*_{Plug}$. (Hint: $z$ for $CR=0.8$ is $\approx 0.84$).
  4. Compare $Q^*_{SAA}$ and $Q^*_{Plug}$. Discuss the risks of the plug-in method.
